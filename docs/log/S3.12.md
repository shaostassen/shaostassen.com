## The one to try

**Set a disturbance and flip between RS-232 and RS-485.** Four volts of induced
noise destroys RS-232 and the identical disturbance is invisible to RS-485,
because it lands on both wires equally and cancels in the subtraction. That one
fact is why one reaches 15 m and the other 1200, and it is the best single
demonstration in the whole instrument.

It only works because RS-232 is modelled honestly. The standard permits ±5 to
±15 V, but the charge-pump drivers actually fitted produce about ±5.5 — so the
margin against a ±3 V threshold is **2.5 V, not 12**. Modelling the generous end
would have flattered RS-232 with headroom real hardware does not have, and the
comparison would have been a lie that happened to look good.

## The others

- **CAN** — non-destructive arbitration. Everybody transmits at once, the wire
  is a wired-AND, and a node that sends recessive while reading dominant
  withdraws mid-frame *without disturbing a bit of the winner's message*. A test
  asserts the contested frame is delivered byte-identically to the uncontested
  one, which is the whole claim. Plus bit stuffing, and the ACK slot that means
  a CAN node alone on a bus cannot transmit at all.
- **1-Wire** — the bit is the *width* of the low pulse, so time carries the
  data. Real DS18B20 timings on a 10 µs grid, drawn to scale, so the 480 µs
  reset visibly costs about as much as seven data bits.
- **Manchester** — a line code, labelled as such. An edge in every cell buys
  clock recovery and DC balance at exactly twice the signalling rate. Its
  failure is level 01's polarity trap one layer up: IEEE 802.3 and G. E. Thomas
  are exact inverses, and an inverted Manchester signal is still valid
  Manchester.
- **USB** — NRZI inverts the intuition (a *zero* flips the line) and, with one
  stuffed bit per six ones, bounds how long the line can sit still at seven bit
  times. The same guarantee Manchester pays double the bandwidth for, bought far
  more cheaply. And the host schedules everything, which is why an absent device
  is a *timeout* rather than a collision.

## Engine and renderer notes

`SignalTrace` gained an optional `volts` array, and `BusScope` draws analogue
signals as real voltages against a range with a zero line. RS-232 and RS-485 are
arguments about voltage; squaring them into levels would have hidden the point.

The RS-232 receiver also holds its last state in the undefined zone between
thresholds rather than defaulting to mark. Without that, a disturbance that
pushed the line into no-man's-land resolved in the transmitter's favour and the
model reported success where real hardware would be guessing — a test caught it.

## Defect found

Two tests failed on first run and both were my claims, not the code. The
RS-485-rejects-16 V test was wrong: 16 V genuinely *is* outside the −7…+12 V
common-mode range, so RS-485 should fail there. Fixing it meant separating "a
disturbance it rejects" (4 V) from "a disturbance beyond its range" (40 V), and
the second is now its own test — differential signalling is not magic, and
pinning where it gives up is more honest than pretending it does not.

## Problem

Voice-cloning demos are easy to make and hard to trust: the sample that gets
posted is the one that came out well. I wanted to clone a voice from a few
seconds of audio and, more than that, to be able to say whether it had worked —
as a number a stranger could reproduce.

The motivating application is pronunciation. Synthesize a sentence in a native
speaker's voice, convert it onto the learner's own, and let the learner hear
themselves saying it correctly.

## Constraints

- CPU only. Every number comes from one Ryzen 9 9950X with no GPU, the
  [remote box](/projects/ml-workstation-edge-pipeline) that also ran
  [SpeechLens](/projects/speechlens).
- The repo is public, so the models have to be usable in public. For cloning
  models the restriction often lives in the weights rather than the code, so
  every model's code and weight licences are audited and the excluded ones are
  listed with the reason.
- A result is not recorded unless its hardware, model, date and raw log are.

## Approach

Two cloning backends, Chatterbox and CosyVoice 2, sit behind one interface,
with Piper as a non-autoregressive control. Intelligibility is scored by two
independent recognisers, Whisper and a HuBERT-CTC model, and speaker similarity
by an encoder. SpeechLens supplies the recogniser and its per-word confidence.

Every table carries a ground-truth row: the real recording, put through the
same scorer. It bounds what any other number can mean. Synthesis is seeded by
default, and the core install can score a directory of wav files without a TTS
stack. 155 tests pass offline with no weights and no network.

## Why it's technically hard

The measurement is easier to get wrong than the synthesis.

My biggest finding is a mistake of mine. LibriSpeech and EdAcc ship transcripts
as unpunctuated `ALL CAPS`, a convention for scoring, and I passed them to a
generative model as though they were input. On LibriSpeech test-clean at n=60,
Chatterbox scored 0.112 WER on the raw capitals and 0.022 in sentence case,
same utterances, against 0.021 for the real recordings. Changing case and
adding a full stop cut the error fivefold, and it refuted a README claim that
the gap was real. What made it findable was the ground-truth row reproducing
the published 2.1%: the harness had been measuring correctly all along, so the
anomaly had to be my input.

The effect looks specific to autoregressive models. In a paired study, same
utterance and seed with only the case differing, Chatterbox went from 0.109 to
0.025 (sign test p = 1e-7). Piper, which emits in one pass, went from 0.051 to
0.036 (p = 0.36). Runaway generation appeared only in autoregressive decoders
and only on capitals.

The second backend's first score, 0.212 against 0.022, was also partly mine. I
declined to publish that ranking, because its 10 s reference cap came from the
vendor's documentation rather than a measurement. Where the cap truncated the
reference, WER was 0.507 (n=25); where it did not, 0.033 (n=35). Without
truncation CosyVoice 2 scores 0.036.

That was the fourth constant I took from documentation that did not transfer,
after a published similarity ceiling (0.69–0.76 in papers, 0.89–0.98 on valid
same-speaker pairs here), a vendor real-time claim that was about 6× off, and
the same cap on the first backend. The other three errors came from studies
that printed a plausible table while hiding their own gap.

## Result

Each backend at a measured reference cap lands essentially on the real
recordings: 0.022 WER for Chatterbox and 0.036 for CosyVoice 2, against 0.021,
with speaker similarity 0.949 and 0.958 against 0.964. That is a trade-off, not
a ranking. It is one corpus, one language, n=60.

On accented speech (12 EdAcc speakers, n=72) the conversion stage does not
damage the words: 0.073 WER against 0.076 for the native synthesis it started
from, and 0.087 for plain cloning. It is partial, not a clean transfer. Scored
against the source speaker's real audio, the output sits 65% of the way toward
the learner and 56% toward the source.

Correcting only the flagged words keeps the voice and loses the words.
Regenerating 19.5% of the audio retains 99% of the identity ceiling, but WER
rises to 0.253 from 0.176 for the untouched recording. Damage tracks how much
is replaced (correlation +0.675), not how many seams there are (+0.109), so the
crossfades are fine and the fragments are the problem.

One retraction is worth naming. Comparing against the source's synthesis rather
than its real audio flatters the result by +0.070, because the conversion
shares its vocoder and prosody. It pushed a per-utterance rate from 44% to 54%
and produced a "majority blend" verdict that I retracted. The function that
computes it now refuses the unmatched input.

## What I'd do next

Synthesize the whole utterance, then splice in only the corrected region, since
two words generated alone get no context to fit the gap. The two recognisers
are not independent on LibriSpeech, where HuBERT is trained, so the pair
agrees there partly through domain match. And one non-autoregressive model is
not a family: the architecture claim needs more than Piper before I would state
it as general.
